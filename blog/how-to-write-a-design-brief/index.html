How to Write a Design Brief (Free Template)

nov 5, 2026

A design brief is a short document that tells a designer what you need: who you are, who it's for, what the project should achieve, what you like and don't like, and your budget and deadline. It doesn't need to be long or perfect — one or two pages is enough. A free template is at the end of this article.

Why a brief matters

A good brief saves time and money for both sides. The designer understands the task faster, makes fewer wrong guesses, and you get fewer "nice, but not us" moments.

It also helps you. Writing a brief forces you to think about your goals before spending money — and makes it easier to compare offers from different designers.

What to include in a design brief

1. About your business

What you do, how long you've been doing it and what makes you different. Add your story if it matters — why you started is often the most useful part for a designer.

2. The project

What exactly you need: a logo, a full brand identity, a landing page, a website, or all of it. Is it a new brand or a redesign? If it's a redesign, what's wrong with the current one?

3. Goals

What should the new design achieve? More bookings, higher prices, a new audience, a product launch? Be specific: "look more premium so we can raise prices by 20%" is far more useful than "look better".

4. Audience

Who are your customers? Age, location, what they care about, where else they shop. The same design can work brilliantly for one audience and fail for another.

5. Feeling and style

How should people feel when they see your brand? Try to pick three words — for example calm, warm, natural or bold, smart, fast.

6. References

5–10 examples you like — websites, brands, packaging, anything — with one line on what you like about each. Plus 2–3 things you don't like or want to avoid.

7. Competitors

2–3 competitors and what you think of their brand: what's good and what you want to do differently.

8. Content and materials

What you already have: logo, photos, texts, brand colors. What still needs to be created — and by whom.

9. Budget and timeline

Even a rough range helps. A realistic budget and deadline let the designer suggest the best option for you, instead of guessing.

10. Decision-maker

Who gives final approval? One clear decision-maker makes projects much faster.

Common brief mistakes

  • Describing the design instead of the goal. "Blue, with a big photo" is less useful than "we want families to trust us".
  • Using vague words without examples. "Modern" and "clean" mean different things to different people. Add references.
  • Hiding the budget. It doesn't make the price lower — it just makes the offer less accurate.
  • Writing a novel. A 15-page brief usually means nobody reads it carefully. One or two pages is enough.
  • Forgetting what you don't want. Often it's faster to say "definitely not this" than to describe the perfect result.
